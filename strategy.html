Nosebleed CapitalStrategy

The whole trade: get in at face value through a channel most buyers can't use, get out on the open resale market once real demand is priced in. Everything below is the checklist for finding events where that gap is real, and the exit rules for when it isn't.

The core trade

Buy in the primary market at face value. Sell in the secondary market once scarcity is priced in. The edge isn't taste or luck — it's access. Most tickets never touch resale at a premium because most buyers pay whatever the primary market charges, at whatever time slot they can get. The trade only works when there's a legitimate way to buy before, or instead of, the general public — and a legitimate way to resell after.

Primary-side access channels

  • Amex Card Member presales. Most events with an Amex partnership (US Open, F1 Miami/Vegas, various concerts and majors) open a presale window to any Amex Card Member — Platinum, not Centurion, is what unlocks this. Centurion adds concierge-sourced access to separate, already-scarce VIP inventory at a $10k init + $5k/yr cost; it's not a better version of the same presale and isn't worth carrying for this strategy's margins.
  • Official lotteries / ballots. Masters badges, Wimbledon's public ballot, and some Ryder Cup allocations are lottery-based rather than first-come presales. These get face-value access without needing any card affiliation — but check the resale terms before assuming a ballot win is tradeable (see Exclusion criteria below).
  • Cardmember/loyalty on-sale windows from the venue, league, or ticketing platform directly (e.g. season-ticket-holder presales, team credit union or bank partnerships) — same shape as Amex, different gatekeeper.

Secondary-side exit

Sell through an official or verified resale channel (e.g. Ticketmaster Verified Resale) rather than an informal one, and price off sold comps, not asking prices. Two numbers matter and they are not the same thing: the seller's take-home price and the buyer's paid price differ by the platform's fee stack (e.g. Ticketmaster: buyer pays seller price × ~1.165, seller keeps seller price × 0.90). Set the listing price by working backward from a buyer-facing comp, not by copying it.

Screening a new event

Before sizing a position, an event needs to clear all four:

1. Face-value access existsAmex presale, official lottery, or another primary-side channel you can actually use — not just "tickets exist," but a window before or outside the general public sale.
2. Resale is allowedCheck the actual terms, not the vibe. If the issuer prohibits resale of the ticket/badge, this isn't a trade — it's a rules violation with a nice spread attached.
3. Scarcity tends to hold, not decayLook for a track record (or a clear structural reason) that secondary prices hold up or rise into the event, not a novelty spike that fades in later years.
4. There's tier/session variance to exploitMultiple sessions, seat tiers, or matchup-dependent slots create the mispricing. A single flat-price ticket type gives you nothing to arbitrage.

Exclusion criteria — skip regardless of spread size

Resale contractually prohibitedMasters badges are the clear case: Augusta National bans resale outright. A $2,000–$8,700 street price doesn't matter if the position can just be voided or the account banned. Treat "prohibited" as a hard no, not a risk to size around.
Documented price-decay patternF1 Las Vegas GP: 2023 inaugural prices as high as $1,387 the night before, collapsed to roughly $477–$509 by 2024, with 2025 face prices cut in response. That's hype decaying, not scarcity building — the opposite of what this strategy needs. Same failure mode hit PGA Championship tickets, which dropped below face on resale despite selling out. Don't infer a spread from face-vs-asking-price alone; check whether the event's history runs toward or away from face value.
Asking price mistaken for sold priceAggregator "starting at" prices are the cheapest live listing, not a transacted comp. Confirm with actual sold history before treating a gap as real.

Position & exit rules

  1. Size across sessions/tiers, not into one. Multiple pairs in the same section compete with each other on the way out. Spread capital across sessions or days with independent demand drivers (different matchups, different nights).
  2. Reprice on real signal. Raise the ask if a session's matchup/lineup improves (star players advancing, a marquee pairing), cut if it doesn't. Don't anchor to a static estimate made at purchase time.
  3. List 7–14 days out. Early enough to catch buyers planning ahead, late enough that real scarcity (or its absence) is visible.
  4. Cut morning-of rather than hold to expiry if unsold — a ticket worth something below cost basis beats one worth zero after the event passes.
  5. Work backward from buyer price to seller price using the platform's actual fee split before setting a list price.
  6. Log every trade in the trade log — position, P&L, what actually happened vs. the pre-trade estimate, and a playbook update for next year. The pattern only compounds if the misses get written down as carefully as the wins.
