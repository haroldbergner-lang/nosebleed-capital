Nosebleed CapitalStrategy

The whole trade: get in at face value through a channel most buyers can't use, get out on the open resale market once real demand is priced in.

The core trade

Buy in the primary market at face value. Sell in the secondary market once scarcity is priced in. The edge isn't taste or luck — it's access. Most tickets never touch resale at a premium because most buyers pay whatever the primary market charges, at whatever time slot they can get. The trade only works when there's a legitimate way to buy before, or instead of, the general public — and a legitimate way to resell after.

Primary-side access channels

  • Amex Presale Tickets®. Early access before general on-sale — this is the actual mechanism behind the US Open trade: buying before the public even can, ahead of demand getting priced in. Platinum, not Centurion, is what unlocks this. Centurion adds concierge-sourced access to separate, already-scarce VIP inventory at a $10k init + $5k/yr cost; it's not a better version of the same presale and isn't worth carrying for this strategy's margins.
  • Amex Reserved Tickets® — a distinct, weaker tier. This is a dedicated allotment reserved for Card Members, not necessarily an early window ahead of the public. It's an exclusive channel, not inherently a mispricing window, and it's what most Amex-branded corporate hospitality (e.g. F1 Paddock Club) actually is. Don't assume the presale thesis applies just because the badge says "Amex" — check whether it's actually a pre-general-on-sale window or just a walled-off allotment.
  • Premium Events Collection™ — curated, bespoke experiences (dining, entertainment, fashion, sports) for Platinum and Centurion members. Not resellable tickets. Out of scope for this strategy entirely.
  • Official lotteries / ballots. Masters badges, Wimbledon's public ballot, and some Ryder Cup allocations are lottery-based rather than first-come presales. These get face-value access without needing any card affiliation — but check the resale terms before assuming a ballot win is tradeable (e.g. Masters badges cannot legally be resold).
  • Cardmember/loyalty on-sale windows from the venue, league, or ticketing platform directly (e.g. season-ticket-holder presales, team credit union or bank partnerships) — same shape as Amex, different gatekeeper.

Other bank/network presale programs — Amex isn't the only one

  • Citi Entertainment / Citi presale. Any Citi-issued Visa or Mastercard credit card, plus Citibank Mastercard debit cards. Access via the first 6 digits of the card number at Ticketmaster/Live Nation checkout, or by linking accounts ahead of time. The deepest channel for Live Nation/Ticketmaster concerts specifically — broader concert coverage than Amex.
  • Mastercard Priceless® presale. Network-level, not issuer-specific — any consumer Mastercard credit card qualifies, regardless of which bank issued it. Presale window runs up to ~48 hours before general on-sale. World Elite/World Legend tier cards get an upgraded "Preferred Tickets" allocation on top of the base presale.
  • Chase Cardholder Presales / Chase Preferred Seats. Any Chase-issued card, but the coverage is narrow — concentrated at Madison Square Garden–owned venues (MSG, Radio City, Beacon Theatre). Worth it specifically for those rooms, not a general-purpose sourcing channel.
  • Capital One Entertainment. Eligible Capital One rewards cards get presale access via an iHeartRadio partnership and the Capital One Entertainment portal, plus expedited entry at Capital One Arena. Smaller program, also stacks a cashback rate on the ticket purchase itself.
  • Visa Signature/Infinite entertainment offers. Mostly a 2-for-1 ticket discount and concierge-arranged tickets, not a timing-based presale. Different mechanism — a cost-reduction lever, not a "beat the public to it" edge — but useful for lowering cost basis when access is already secured some other way.
Cards on hand

Citi Double Cash — no annual fee, but it's been issued as a World Elite Mastercard since 2020, which stacks two independent channels: Citi Entertainment (issuer-level) and Mastercard Priceless, including the upgraded Preferred Tickets tier that base Mastercards don't get. This is the primary sourcing card — check Citi Entertainment and priceless.com/music before assuming a new candidate needs Amex.

Chase Sapphire Preferred — Visa Signature (not Infinite), so no network-level presale timing edge. Useful for Chase Preferred Seats if something lands at an MSG-owned venue, and for the Visa Signature 2-for-1 offer as a cost-reduction stack, not as a primary sourcing channel.

Secondary-side exit

Sell through an official or verified resale channel (e.g. Ticketmaster Verified Resale) rather than an informal one, and price off sold comps, not asking prices. Two numbers matter and they are not the same thing: the seller's take-home price and the buyer's paid price differ by the platform's fee stack (e.g. Ticketmaster: buyer pays seller price × ~1.165, seller keeps seller price × 0.90). Set the listing price by working backward from a buyer-facing comp, not by copying it.

Primary isn't always Ticketmaster

"Primary" means whoever sold the ticket on behalf of the venue/promoter at face value; "secondary" means an individual reselling a ticket they already hold. Ticketmaster (Live Nation) handles most major US concert/arena primary ticketing, but it's not universal — the primary platform is set by the venue's own contract, not by the artist or tour, so a single tour can run on different rails city to city. Rush's tour is mostly Ticketmaster, but Houston's Toyota Center presale ran through AXS (AEG's competing platform) instead. Other primary providers to expect at smaller or non-arena venues: SeatGeek Enterprise, Etix, See Tickets, DICE, Paciolan, Eventbrite. Each platform runs its own separate official resale marketplace (Ticketmaster Verified Resale, AXS Official Resale) and its own separate fraud/anti-scalping detection — a purchase pattern on one platform doesn't carry over to another, since they're independent systems with independent purchase histories.

On an event page, tell primary from resale by: a "Resale"/"Verified Resale" tag on the listing, and price shape — primary tickets follow a small number of fixed tiers set by the venue, while resale prices vary individually by seller. A banner stating "primary tickets are extremely limited or have sold out" is the platform telling you directly that everything showing is now resale.
